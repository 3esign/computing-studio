Collecting data.
Curating what we find.

The groups have chosen their objects and research tracks; the data is being collected. At the consultations, bring what you found as a data map — a first system among your elements: relevance, importance, accuracy, source, hierarchy. What you could not find yet is recorded as unknown — that is a result too.

The consultation task

One data map per object.

Show the collected material as a map, a scheme or a system of elements — digital, drawn or collage; the medium is yours. This is not a finished survey: introduce a first order among your elements, and that order becomes the starting matrix for everything collected next. Material stays in your archive even when you do not show it — and if something is interesting, or its use is unclear, bring that too.

  1. Relevance

    Does the element speak about your object through your chosen track? What serves a different question waits politely aside — it is not thrown away, it is simply not in the first plan.

  2. Importance

    Ask which elements carry the most: the span of a bridge, the author's decision, the story the city tells. The carriers sit in the first plan; everything else supports them.

  3. Accuracy

    Three states, always named: verified, reported, unknown. Two sources that disagree are not a problem to hide — the disagreement is itself a finding.

  4. Source

    Every element names where it came from: author, date, exact place. A source is part of the data; without it, the element is only a rumour.

  5. Hierarchy

    Order the map: what leads, what supports, what waits. A beginner's map can be five honest elements with sources — worth more than fifty piled-up clippings.

When a source is new to you, check it the same four ways every time: investigate who stands behind it; find a second source and compare; trace the claim to its original context; and stop when you have enough to act.

Finding sources

Where new data hides.

The first source is rarely the last. An object unfolds through many materialities: newspaper articles and archives, books and published plans, exhibitions and other cultural events, competitions if there were any — and the object itself: a visit, your own photographs, people who remember it. Ask around; the point is a verified base, not a fast one.

The digital twin

From archive to twin.

The semester works on the digital twin: creating a digital copy of a physical object — as if bringing the object as close as possible to someone who has never seen it, never walked through it: its materials, its shape, the stories, its life. The first step on that road is data curation: notice the information and its source, collect it, evaluate it, and arrange it into a wider system of connected information with hierarchy.

You can experiment at aistudio.google.com: upload your collected data (the + button) and explain in the prompt that you are building a digital passport — a digital twin — of your chosen building, and that you want to try different ways of presenting and experiencing it: everything that belongs to the object and is not its physically present materiality.
